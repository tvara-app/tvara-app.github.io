How much of your AI usage limit is left, and why the app will not say

You are mid-task and the answer stops. "You have reached your limit." No warning, no countdown, and often no clear statement of when it lifts.

Why limits are invisible

Providers meter usage on rolling windows — typically a few hours, and a longer weekly one — and they mostly do not surface the counter. There are real reasons for it. The limit is often not a simple message count but a weighted allowance where a long answer with tool use costs more than a short reply, so any number shown would be approximate and would invite arguments. The thresholds also move; publishing one invites complaints when it changes.

The result is that the one number you actually need — how much is left before you get cut off — is the number you cannot see.

What the platforms do publish

More than they show you. The same endpoints their own interfaces call often carry usage figures that are simply never rendered:

  • Some expose a percentage of a window spent, plus the time it resets.
  • Some report a plan tier, which is the only reliable statement of which limits apply to you at all.
  • Some meter side features — deep research, image generation, voice — separately from the main allowance, which is why you can hit one wall while another is untouched.
  • At least one publishes nothing whatsoever, and enforces purely by refusing requests. For that one, any number is a guess.

What you can do without any tools

  1. Know your window. Most limits are rolling, not daily. If you were cut off at 2pm on a five-hour window, you recover progressively from 7pm, not at midnight.
  2. Front-load the expensive work. Long context, file analysis and tool use consume allowance faster than short questions. Do them at the start of a window.
  3. Split across platforms deliberately. If you have access to more than one assistant, put routine work on one and keep the strongest allowance for work that needs it.
  4. Watch for the slowdown before the wall. Some providers degrade to a smaller model before refusing outright. If answers suddenly get shorter and worse, you are close.
